Right now I'm running three D&D campaigns, and all three take place in the same world I've been building for years.

The first one I started is the main thread. The third campaign takes place on a different continent of that same world, at the same point in time. Same world, same timeline, two groups of players who have never met. What one party does can reach the other, because they share a single reality.

The second campaign is the odd one out. It runs the same storyline as the first, the same core problems, the same villains, but it isn't the same world. It's a parallel version on its own timeline, with its own group of players. They're facing the same problems the first party faced, but their world is separate. Nothing that happens at one table is canon at the other.

So two of my campaigns share a world, and one of them just shares a story. That's the setup. The part I actually care about is what I let move between them.

## Creativity Crosses, Consequences Don't

When a player at one table builds something I love, I carry it into the other worlds. A society, a family, a piece of someone's backstory, a thing they did that I can't stop thinking about. If it's good, I don't want it to exist in only one place. I don't have a system for deciding what makes the jump. I just do what's cool.

The one thing I never carry is consequence. The ideas cross. The outcomes stay where they happened.

People hear "parallel worlds running the same story" and assume I'm building a multiverse. I'm not, and I think the distinction matters. Most multiverses work like this: every possibility exists somewhere, and every choice branches into a world where you chose differently. It sounds epic, but it kills the thing I care about most. If there's a world for every outcome, then there are infinite worlds to save and none of them matter, because somewhere out there is a version where you already won, or already failed, or never had to choose at all. The stakes dissolve.

I like stakes. I like tragedy. I like it when a choice closes a door for good. My worlds aren't branches of each other. The second campaign isn't "the timeline where the first party chose differently." It's a separate world that happens to face the same problems. A death at one table isn't softened by the fact that the same character is alive at the other, because they were never the same person in the same world to begin with.

So what crosses between my worlds isn't what the players win or lose. It's what they make.

## The Shapeshifters

One of my players in the first campaign wanted a very specific backstory. He grew up in a society of shapeshifters, the kind that lives quietly inside a big city and runs a lot of it from behind the scenes. Political influence, a little espionage, hands in places nobody would expect. He wanted most of this hidden from the rest of the party, which only made it better. So I built the society out: who they are, how they operate, what they want, who they've already got their hooks into.

It was too good to leave in one world. The same society exists in the second campaign's world now, sitting under the surface of a city there too. That party has already brushed up against it. They just don't realize what they've found yet. They're pulling on a thread without knowing where it leads, and the thread happens to be one a player at a completely different table spun for me.

The two cities aren't the same city. The two parties will never meet. But the same idea is alive in both places, doing different things depending on who stumbles into it.

## Two Druids, One Family

This one started as a coincidence and turned into my favorite example of how this works.

In the first campaign, a player wanted to be a Circle of the Moon druid with a grove and a family behind him. I built all of it: the family, where they came from, the little society around the grove. Standard backstory work.

Then in the second campaign, a different player came to me wanting something really similar. Circle of the Moon, a grove, a family. But his take on the character was almost the opposite. He wanted to be estranged from them, the black sheep, chaotic in his own way. The pieces fit together so well that I asked him a question: what if the two of you were brothers?

He went for it. So now I have two druids at two different tables who are brothers, and neither player has ever met the other. They hear about each other in pieces. A mention here, a rumor there. Both of them are chaotic, in completely different directions. I didn't plan any of it. One player built half of it, another player built the other half, and all I did was notice they lined up and ask the question.

## The Crab

My favorite thing that's ever crossed over is a giant crab.

In the second campaign, the party found an underground fighting ring where monsters were pitted against each other for sport. At their druid's urging, they decided to free the captive animals, and deep in the place they found an enormous crab. Getting it out was its own ordeal. They managed it by turning the thing into gaseous form, a literal crabby mist, after a fight they barely walked away from. Then they teleported out, landed near a fishing village, and went to sleep. They left a giant crab loose next to a town full of people. The crab did what a giant crab does. The village didn't make it.

That crab exists on the third campaign's continent too. There it got loose near a fishing town the third party had actually visited, a place they knew. They heard what happened. They saw what was left. The crab is still out there on their coastline, and they may well go looking for it.

Remember, the second and third campaigns aren't the same world. So this isn't the same crab finishing a journey. It's the same crab loose in two separate realities, eating two separate towns. Its statblock came across. Its story came across. If the third party ever corners it and tries to talk it down, whatever it is underneath all that carnage will be there too. The crab those players meet is the crab another table set loose, even though those two tables will never compare notes.

## Where It Gets Tricky

The further something gets from "an idea" and the closer it gets to "a specific character," the more careful I have to be. That's where I risk building the exact multiverse I just said I hate.

Take the two druid brothers. If one of them ever turns up in the other's world, he crosses as a personality and nothing else. He brings how he talks, how he thinks, the kind of chaos he tends to cause. He does not bring his history, his timeline, or any sense that he's "the same person from the other world." In his new world he's just a guy who happens to be a lot like someone far away. The player's creativity comes across. The continuity does not.

That line is the whole reason this stays fun instead of collapsing into a soup of alternate timelines. The second I let a character be literally the same person across worlds, every world stops mattering, because there's always another copy somewhere. So I keep it to the spark. The personality, the idea, the thing that made me love it in the first place. Never the canon.

I'm also happy to have actual players guest at another table when they want to. That one's less a creative problem and more a logistical one, and mostly it comes down to timing. But the principle is the same. I want the good stuff to travel. I just don't want it dragging a whole second reality along behind it.

## We're All Building It

I've written before that I don't write stories for my players. I build people and problems and let the story happen at the table. Running three campaigns in one world is the same idea, just bigger. The players aren't only building the story of their own campaign. They're building the world all of them are playing in, whether they know it or not.

A society one player invented is pulling strings in a city a different group is about to walk into. A character one player made has a brother at a table he'll never sit at. A crab one party set loose is waiting on a coastline another party calls home. None of that came from me. I just refused to let any of it die when the session ended, and I carried it wherever it was cool to carry it.

That's the part I love most. The world is bigger than any one table because more than one table built it. I'm not the only author. I'm just the one keeping track.
